Hypertension: Clinical Guidelines and Management

Introduction
Hypertension, commonly known as high blood pressure, is one of the most prevalent cardiovascular risk factors worldwide. It is defined as persistently elevated arterial blood pressure, typically systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥80 mmHg.

Classification of Blood Pressure
According to current guidelines:
- Normal: SBP <120 mmHg and DBP <80 mmHg
- Elevated: SBP 120-129 mmHg and DBP <80 mmHg
- Stage 1 Hypertension: SBP 130-139 mmHg or DBP 80-89 mmHg
- Stage 2 Hypertension: SBP ≥140 mmHg or DBP ≥90 mmHg
- Hypertensive Crisis: SBP >180 mmHg and/or DBP >120 mmHg

Epidemiology
Hypertension affects approximately 1.28 billion adults worldwide. The prevalence increases with age, and it is more common in men than women until age 65. Risk factors include obesity, high sodium intake, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol consumption, and genetic predisposition.

Pathophysiology
Blood pressure is determined by cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance. Hypertension develops through complex interactions involving:
- Increased sympathetic nervous system activity
- Activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS)
- Endothelial dysfunction
- Increased sodium retention
- Vascular remodeling

Primary vs Secondary Hypertension

Primary (Essential) Hypertension
Accounts for 90-95% of cases. The exact cause is unknown but involves multiple genetic and environmental factors. It develops gradually over many years.

Secondary Hypertension
Results from an identifiable underlying condition:
- Renal disease (most common cause)
- Renovascular disease
- Primary aldosteronism
- Cushing's syndrome
- Pheochromocytoma
- Coarctation of the aorta
- Obstructive sleep apnea
- Medications (NSAIDs, oral contraceptives, decongestants)

Clinical Presentation
Hypertension is often asymptomatic, earning it the nickname "silent killer." When symptoms occur, they may include:
- Headache (especially occipital, in the morning)
- Dizziness
- Blurred vision
- Shortness of breath
- Nosebleeds
- Chest pain

Target Organ Damage
Chronic hypertension can damage multiple organs:

Cardiovascular System
- Left ventricular hypertrophy
- Heart failure
- Coronary artery disease
- Myocardial infarction
- Arrhythmias

Brain
- Stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic)
- Transient ischemic attacks
- Vascular dementia
- Hypertensive encephalopathy

Kidneys
- Chronic kidney disease
- Proteinuria
- End-stage renal disease

Eyes
- Hypertensive retinopathy
- Retinal hemorrhages
- Vision loss

Peripheral Vasculature
- Peripheral arterial disease
- Aortic aneurysm and dissection

Diagnosis and Evaluation

Blood Pressure Measurement
Proper technique is crucial:
- Patient should be seated quietly for 5 minutes
- Back supported, feet flat on floor
- Arm supported at heart level
- Appropriate cuff size
- Multiple readings on different occasions

Diagnostic Workup
Initial evaluation includes:
- Comprehensive history and physical examination
- Laboratory tests:
  * Complete blood count
  * Basic metabolic panel
  * Lipid profile
  * Urinalysis
  * ECG
  * Estimated glomerular filtration rate
- Consider additional tests based on clinical suspicion

Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring
Provides average BP over 24 hours and identifies:
- White coat hypertension
- Masked hypertension
- Nocturnal hypertension
- BP variability

Management Strategies

Lifestyle Modifications
Essential for all patients:

Diet
- DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension)
- Sodium restriction: <2,300 mg/day, ideally <1,500 mg/day
- Increased potassium intake
- Limited alcohol consumption
- Reduced saturated and trans fats

Physical Activity
- At least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week
- Resistance training 2-3 times per week
- Daily physical activity encouraged

Weight Management
- Target BMI 18.5-24.9 kg/m²
- Even 5-10% weight loss can significantly reduce BP

Stress Management
- Relaxation techniques
- Adequate sleep
- Stress reduction strategies

Pharmacological Treatment

First-Line Antihypertensives
1. Thiazide or Thiazide-like Diuretics
   - Hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone
   - Mechanism: Reduce blood volume

2. ACE Inhibitors
   - Lisinopril, enalapril, ramipril
   - Mechanism: Block conversion of angiotensin I to II

3. Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs)
   - Losartan, valsartan, telmisartan
   - Mechanism: Block angiotensin II receptors

4. Calcium Channel Blockers
   - Amlodipine, diltiazem, verapamil
   - Mechanism: Reduce calcium influx into vascular smooth muscle

Treatment Initiation
- Stage 1 hypertension with ASCVD risk <10%: Lifestyle modifications for 3-6 months
- Stage 1 hypertension with ASCVD risk ≥10%: Lifestyle modifications + medication
- Stage 2 hypertension: Lifestyle modifications + two medications

Additional Agents
- Beta-blockers: Metoprolol, atenolol (specific indications)
- Alpha-blockers: Doxazosin, prazosin
- Direct vasodilators: Hydralazine
- Aldosterone antagonists: Spironolactone, eplerenone

Treatment Goals
General population: <130/80 mmHg
Consider individual factors:
- Age
- Comorbidities
- Risk of adverse effects
- Patient preferences

Special Populations

Pregnancy
- Chronic hypertension predates pregnancy
- Gestational hypertension develops after 20 weeks
- Preeclampsia: Hypertension with proteinuria
- Management: Methyldopa, labetalol, nifedipine
- Avoid ACE inhibitors and ARBs (teratogenic)

Elderly Patients
- Higher prevalence of isolated systolic hypertension
- Increased risk of orthostatic hypotension
- Start low, go slow with medications
- Target: <130/80 mmHg if tolerated

Diabetes
- Higher cardiovascular risk
- Target: <130/80 mmHg
- ACE inhibitors or ARBs preferred (renoprotective)

Chronic Kidney Disease
- Target: <130/80 mmHg
- ACE inhibitors or ARBs for proteinuria
- May require multiple agents

Hypertensive Emergencies
BP ≥180/120 mmHg with acute target organ damage:
- Hypertensive encephalopathy
- Acute myocardial infarction
- Acute heart failure
- Aortic dissection
- Stroke

Management:
- Immediate hospitalization
- IV antihypertensive therapy
- Reduce BP by 10-15% in first hour
- Avoid excessive BP reduction

Monitoring and Follow-up
- Initial follow-up: 1 month after starting treatment
- Stable patients: Every 3-6 months
- Monitor for:
  * BP control
  * Medication adherence
  * Side effects
  * Target organ damage
  * Electrolyte abnormalities

Patient Education
Key topics:
- Understanding hypertension and its risks
- Proper home BP monitoring technique
- Medication compliance
- Lifestyle modifications
- Recognition of warning signs
- Importance of regular follow-up

Prognosis
With appropriate treatment, cardiovascular risk can be significantly reduced. However, hypertension remains a leading cause of:
- Stroke
- Heart attack
- Heart failure
- Kidney disease
- Premature death

Resistant Hypertension
Defined as uncontrolled BP despite:
- Three or more medications (including a diuretic)
- All at optimal doses

Causes:
- Non-adherence
- Improper BP measurement
- Secondary causes
- Interfering substances
- Volume overload

Management:
- Assess adherence
- Screen for secondary causes
- Consider specialist referral
- Add mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist

Conclusion
Hypertension is a major public health challenge requiring comprehensive management. Early detection, lifestyle modifications, and appropriate pharmacological therapy can prevent serious cardiovascular complications. A patient-centered approach with regular monitoring and adjustment of therapy is essential for optimal outcomes.
